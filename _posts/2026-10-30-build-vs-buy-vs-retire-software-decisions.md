"Should we build it or buy it?" is one of the most common technology questions in any growing company. It's a good question, but it's incomplete. It assumes the software should exist at all.

A better framework has three options: **build, buy, or retire**.

## Start with the problem, not the solution

Before comparing options, write down in one or two sentences what business problem the software solves and what it's worth to solve it. If you can't, stop there. That's usually a sign the third option is the right one.

## When to buy

Buy an existing product when:

- The problem is common to many businesses, such as accounting, CRM, HR, scheduling, or ticketing
- A mature product handles at least most of your needs well
- Your process can reasonably adapt to how the product works
- The vendor is stable and the product is actively maintained

Buying means someone else carries the maintenance, security, and platform changes. That's usually worth a lot. The trade-offs are subscription cost, less control, and dependence on the vendor's roadmap.

## When to build

Build custom software when:

- The capability is genuinely part of how you compete or deliver value
- No product fits without contorting your business
- You need deep integration across systems in ways products can't support
- You're prepared to own it after launch

That last point is the one most often skipped. Custom software has to be monitored, patched, documented, and changed for as long as you use it. (See [The Hidden Costs of Custom Software After Launch](/blog/hidden-costs-of-custom-software-after-launch/).) Building without a plan for ownership is how orphaned software gets created.

## When to retire

Retire, meaning stop doing the thing or stop using the tool, when:

- Usage is low or declining
- Another system already covers the need well enough
- The process it supports no longer matters as much as it once did
- The cost of maintaining it, in money, risk, and attention, exceeds its value

Retirement is the most underused option because nobody gets credit for removing things. But every system you retire is one less thing to secure, patch, pay for, and explain on a security questionnaire.

## A quick decision guide

| Question | If yes |
|---|---|
| Is this a common business function? | Lean toward **buy** |
| Is it core to how we compete? | Consider **build** |
| Will we commit to owning it long-term? | Required to **build** |
| Is anyone really using it? If not | **Retire** |
| Does something we already have cover it? | **Consolidate** or **retire** |

## The principle behind it

The best software portfolios aren't the most advanced. They're the smallest that do the job well, with every remaining system clearly owned and properly maintained.
