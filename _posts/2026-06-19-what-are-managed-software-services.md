Most established companies already understand managed IT. A provider watches the network, patches laptops, handles the help desk, and sends one predictable invoice. Nobody expects the office manager to apply firewall updates.

The software that actually runs the business is a different story. The custom billing integration, the customer portal a contractor built in 2019, the scheduling app operations depends on, the scripts that move data between systems every night. In most companies, nobody is responsible for keeping those healthy. They work until they don't.

**Managed software services** close that gap. It's the same model as managed IT, applied to your applications.

## What a managed software service covers

A managed software provider takes ongoing ownership of the applications, integrations, and internal tools your business depends on. In practice that means:

- **Monitoring and incident response.** Uptime and error alerts that reach a named engineer, so you find out about problems before customers do.
- **Patching and dependency updates.** Frameworks, libraries, and runtimes kept current on a schedule rather than after a breach.
- **Security scanning.** Checking code and configuration for exposed secrets, vulnerable packages, and risky settings.
- **Backups and recovery.** Verified backups, plus restores that are actually tested.
- **Documentation and runbooks.** Written knowledge that belongs to your company instead of living in one person's head.
- **Changes and enhancements.** Bug fixes and feature requests handled by someone who knows the codebase.
- **Access and key custody.** Credentials, domains, and vendor accounts inventoried and controlled.
- **Portfolio reviews.** A regular look at what you run, what it costs, and what to consolidate.

## How it differs from managed IT

Managed IT focuses on infrastructure and end users: devices, networks, email, identity, the help desk. Those are largely standardized, so a provider can manage hundreds of similar environments the same way.

Business applications aren't standardized. Every custom system has its own code, data model, integrations, and quirks. Managing them requires software engineering skills: reading code, deploying changes safely, understanding how data flows between systems. That's why most IT providers stop at the application boundary, and why so many companies have well-managed laptops and unmanaged software.

## How it differs from hiring a dev shop

A development agency is usually organized around projects: scope it, build it, invoice it, move on. That's useful when you need something new. But software spends most of its life *after* launch, and that's exactly when project-based relationships end.

A managed software service is organized around ownership. The goal isn't to ship more features; it's to keep what you already rely on healthy, documented, and improving, and often to reduce how many systems you run.

## Who it's for

Managed software services make the most sense for companies that:

- Depend on custom or legacy software but don't have an in-house engineering team
- Had the person who built a critical system move on
- Face security questionnaires, audits, or insurance renewals that ask questions nobody can answer
- Have accumulated overlapping tools and integrations over the years
