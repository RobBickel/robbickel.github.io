Most companies don't have a software problem. They have a **visibility** problem. Somewhere between the third internal tool and the fifth "temporary" integration, they lost track of what they actually own.

A **software portfolio audit** fixes that. Here's what one covers.

## 1. A complete inventory

Every application, integration, script, automation, dashboard, and spreadsheet that does more than a spreadsheet should. For each one:

- **What it does:** stated purpose versus actual use
- **Who depends on it:** users, downstream systems, and business processes
- **What it costs:** licensing, hosting, maintenance time, and coordination overhead
- **Who maintains it:** or the fact that nobody does

## 2. A dependency map

Systems don't exist in isolation. The audit traces:

- How data flows between systems
- Integration points and how they fail
- Shared credentials and access patterns
- Single points of failure

This is usually where the surprises are, such as the nightly script that three departments unknowingly depend on.

## 3. A risk assessment

Not all technical debt is equal. The audit identifies:

- **Key-person dependencies:** systems that stop being changeable when one person leaves
- **Undocumented logic:** business rules buried in code nobody reads
- **Security exposure:** credentials in repositories, outdated dependencies, missing access controls
- **Compliance gaps:** what would fail a security review or audit tomorrow

## 4. A prioritized action plan

A list of problems isn't useful on its own. The audit ends with recommendations ranked by risk and return:

- What to **keep:** it's working, so leave it alone
- What to **consolidate:** several tools doing one job
- What to **rebuild:** critical but too fragile
- What to **retire:** nobody will miss it

## How the process works

**Weeks 1–2, discovery.** Stakeholder interviews, documentation review, and access to systems. The goal is the full picture, including what's actually running rather than what's on the org chart.

**Weeks 2–4, analysis.** Inventory, dependency tracing, risk assessment, and identifying redundancy.

**Weeks 4–6, synthesis.** Recommendations, cost estimates, and prioritization, delivered as an executive brief plus a technical appendix.

**Readout.** A working session with leadership to walk through findings and next steps.

## What happens after

You have three options:

1. **Execute internally.** The documentation is yours and is structured for handoff.
2. **Bring in help to execute,** including ongoing ownership through [managed software services](/).
3. **Do nothing for now.** At minimum, you know what you're carrying.

The audit is a fixed-scope engagement under a statement of work. If you continue into managed services, it sits under the same enterprise service agreement, with no second procurement cycle.
