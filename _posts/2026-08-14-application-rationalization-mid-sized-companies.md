**Application rationalization** sounds like something only Fortune 500 companies do, with consultants, frameworks, and multi-year roadmaps. But the core idea is simple and arguably matters more for mid-sized companies: decide deliberately which applications you keep, and stop paying, in money and attention, for the rest.

A company with 150 employees and 60 applications doesn't need an enterprise framework. It needs a clear-eyed afternoon with the right list.

## Why mid-sized companies need it most

Large enterprises have architecture teams whose job is to manage the portfolio. Small startups have few systems to begin with. Mid-sized companies are in between: big enough to have accumulated real sprawl, but rarely staffed to manage it.

The result is a portfolio that grew through individual departmental decisions, acquisitions, and contractors, with no one looking at the whole.

## A right-sized approach

### Step 1: Inventory

List every application and record its purpose, owner, users, annual cost, and what it connects to. (See [How to Inventory Your Company's Software](/blog/how-to-inventory-company-software/).)

### Step 2: Score each application on two axes

Keep it simple:

- **Business value:** How important is this to revenue, customers, compliance, or daily operations? (High, medium, or low)
- **Technical health:** Is it supported, secure, documented, and owned? (Good, fair, or poor)

### Step 3: Place each application in a quadrant

| | Good health | Poor health |
|---|---|---|
| **High value** | **Keep and maintain** | **Invest:** modernize or rebuild |
| **Low value** | **Consolidate** into something else | **Retire** |

This one table resolves most decisions quickly.

### Step 4: Look for functional overlap

Group applications by what they do: communication, project tracking, document storage, reporting, CRM. Wherever there's more than one tool per job, pick a winner.

### Step 5: Sequence the work

Start with retirements, which are quick wins that reduce cost and risk immediately. Then do consolidations. Save rebuilds for last, after you've simplified everything around them.

## Common pitfalls

- **Rationalizing by preference.** "I like this tool better" isn't a reason. Use value and health.
- **Forgetting the data.** Every retirement needs a data export and a plan for where records go.
- **Ignoring integrations.** Removing a system can break something downstream. Map dependencies first.
- **Doing it once.** Without a lightweight review for new tools, sprawl returns within a year or two.
