Many companies buy software help in the least efficient way possible: a new contract for every project, hourly invoices nobody can reconcile, and a fresh round of legal review each time scope changes. It's slow for procurement, frustrating for finance, and it discourages the small, ongoing work that keeps software healthy.

There's a better structure, and it's the one established companies already use for other professional services.

## The two-layer structure

### Layer 1: The service agreement

An **enterprise service agreement**, often called a master service agreement or MSA, sets the terms that don't change from project to project:

- Confidentiality and data protection
- Security obligations
- Intellectual property ownership
- Liability, indemnification, and insurance
- Payment terms and invoicing
- How the relationship can be adjusted or ended

It's negotiated once. After that, new work doesn't restart legal review.

### Layer 2: Statements of work

Each specific piece of work gets a **statement of work** (SOW) under the agreement:

- What's in scope and what isn't
- Deliverables or service levels
- Timeline or term
- How it's priced and billed

SOWs can be added, changed, or retired as needs change. One might cover ongoing management of your customer portal, another a fixed-scope audit, another a specific integration project.

## Why this structure works

- **Faster starts.** New work only needs an SOW, not a new contract.
- **Flexible scope.** Adjusting one application's coverage doesn't touch anything else.
- **Clear ownership.** Each SOW defines exactly what you're getting.
- **Easier procurement.** Vendor onboarding and security review happen once.

## Invoicing your finance team can reconcile

The other half of a good relationship is billing that makes sense:

- **Monthly invoices on standard terms**
- **Purchase-order friendly,** so each SOW can map to a PO
- **Itemized by scope of work,** so you can see what each application or project costs
- **Predictable,** with ongoing services billed consistently rather than as surprise hourly totals

When software costs are itemized per application, portfolio decisions get easier. You can see exactly what it costs to keep each system healthy.

## Questions to ask any software services provider

1. Will you work under a master agreement with separate statements of work?
2. Who owns the code, documentation, and infrastructure you create? (It should be you.)
3. How are invoices structured, and can they map to our purchase orders?
4. Will you complete our vendor onboarding and security review?
5. If we end the relationship, what does handover look like?

If a provider can't answer these clearly, that tells you something.
