When companies budget for custom software, they budget for the build. The proposal has a number, the project ends, and the software goes live.

Then the software keeps existing, and keeps needing attention, for years. The **cost of custom software after launch** is real, predictable, and almost never planned for.

## Where the ongoing costs come from

### Hosting and infrastructure

Servers, databases, storage, domains, email delivery, and monitoring services. These are usually small individually and add up over time, especially when nobody reviews them.

### Security patching

Every application is built on frameworks and libraries that release security updates regularly. Skipping them doesn't save money. It defers the cost and adds risk. Eventually an old dependency develops a known vulnerability or stops working with something else.

### Platform changes you don't control

Browsers change. Operating systems change. The third-party APIs your software calls get new versions, and old ones are retired. Payment processors, mapping services, email providers, and authentication services all evolve, and your software has to keep up.

### Bug fixes and small changes

Real usage reveals edge cases. Business processes shift. Staff ask for small improvements. None of this is a failure of the original build; it's what happens to useful software.

### Knowledge maintenance

If the people who understand the system leave, the next person has to relearn it. Without documentation, that relearning is expensive, and it happens every time someone new touches the code.

### Incidents

Outages, data issues, and integration failures cost money directly in staff time and indirectly in lost revenue and customer trust. Monitoring and backups reduce the cost; ignoring them increases it.

## Why these costs surprise people

Two reasons. First, they're spread out: a little hosting here, a contractor invoice there, a weekend outage once a year. Nobody sees the total. Second, the build team often isn't around to warn you, because project-based relationships end at launch.

## How to plan for it

- **Budget for ongoing care.** Treat maintenance as a line item, not an emergency.
- **Make someone accountable.** An internal owner, an outside partner, or both.
- **Keep dependencies current on a schedule.** Small regular updates are cheaper than big overdue ones.
- **Monitor and back up from day one.** Problems found early are cheap to fix.
- **Document as you go.** A system that's documented is cheaper for anyone to maintain, including a future vendor.
