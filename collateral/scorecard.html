The Software Estate Risk Scorecard

A 34-point assessment of ownership, recoverability, security, lifecycle, cost, and AI across the applications your business runs on.

For COOs, CFOs, CIOs, and the people who keep the systems running

In brief

Most assessments of business software stop at security. This one starts from ownership, because that is where the other risks come from. An application with an accountable owner tends to get patched, backed up, documented, and reviewed. An application without one tends to drift, however well it was built.

The scorecard has 34 items in six dimensions. Each is scored 0, 1, or 2, and each is cross-referenced to the frameworks your auditors, insurers, and customers already use: NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, CIS Critical Security Controls v8.1, ISO/IEC 27001:2022, ITIL 4, DORA and Google SRE practice, TOGAF, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, and ISO/IEC 42001. The full crosswalk is in the appendix.

  • It takes about an hour with a business leader and the most technical person who knows your systems in the same room.
  • It produces a baseline, not a grade. The point is to know where you stand and what to fix first, then to measure progress against your own last score.
  • It highlights four critical items that deserve attention whatever your total.

How to use this scorecard

Choose the scope. Score either your whole software estate or a single business-critical application. If you are unsure where to start, score the application that would hurt most if it stopped working tomorrow: billing, order fulfilment, scheduling, or whatever your customers would notice first. Then score the next one.

Bring two perspectives. A business owner knows what the application must do and what it costs. A technical lead knows how it actually runs. Scoring together surfaces the gaps between those two views, and those gaps are often the most important finding.

Score honestly. Every item uses the same three levels:

0 · Absent. It does not exist, or nobody is sure.

1 · Exists, unproven. It exists, but is informal, incomplete, out of date, or has not been tested.

2 · Owned and proven. It exists, someone is accountable for it, and it has been tested or reviewed in the last 12 months.

“Nobody is sure” scores 0. Uncertainty is itself the finding.

About the bands. The score bands on the results page reflect professional judgment, not an empirically validated threshold. No published research supports a universal cut-off, and Gartner has argued in a research note titled “Technical Debt Benchmarks Are a Waste of Time” that comparisons against other companies mislead.1 Use your score as a baseline and re-score every six to twelve months.

Dimension 1 · Ownership

Ownership is the foundation. The best evidence on key-person risk comes from open-source research: a 2016 study of 133 popular projects found that 65 percent depended on two or fewer people,2 and a study of 1,932 projects found that of those that lost their key developers, only 41 percent survived, all because someone new took ownership.3 With a median tenure of 4.6 years in computer and mathematical occupations,4 most business applications will outlive their builders’ time at the company.

TOGAF’s starting artifact, the Application Portfolio Catalog, exists “to identify and maintain a list of all the applications in the enterprise.”5 Many mid-sized companies cannot produce one. That is where this dimension begins.

Dimension 2 · Operations and recoverability

This dimension separates applications that run from applications that are run. The strongest evidence concerns backups. In a Sophos study of 2,974 organizations hit by ransomware, attackers attempted to compromise backups in 94 percent of attacks. Where backups were compromised, the median recovery cost was $3 million, against $375,000 where they were not.6 Veeam’s 2025 research found that while 98 percent of organizations had a ransomware playbook, only 44 percent included backup verification.7 Both are vendors that sell recovery products, but CISA’s vendor-neutral guidance says the same: “Maintain offline, encrypted backups of critical data, and regularly test the availability and integrity of backups.”8

One point is worth knowing. In the CIS Controls, testing data recovery (safeguard 11.5) sits in Implementation Group 2, above the baseline set that CIS calls essential cyber hygiene.9 NIST CSF 2.0, by contrast, builds testing into the outcome itself: “Backups of data are created, protected, maintained, and tested.”10 Item R3 deliberately sets the higher bar.

For R6, DORA defines five software delivery measures, including change fail rate and failed deployment recovery time.11 If nobody can say what they are for a critical application, that is an ownership finding.

Dimension 3 · Security posture

The security items focus on the hygiene that ownership makes possible. Verizon’s 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report, covering 12,195 confirmed breaches, found credential abuse was the leading way in, at 22 percent of breaches where the entry point was known, and third-party involvement doubled to 30 percent.12 Its 2026 edition reports that the exploitation of vulnerabilities became the top initial access route, at 31 percent.13 Sophos’s 2025 ransomware study, whose sample is weighted toward mid-sized organizations of 100 to 5,000 employees, independently found exploited vulnerabilities to be the most common root cause, at 32 percent.14

Mid-sized companies are not too small to matter. The UK government’s Cyber Security Breaches Survey found that 67 percent of medium-sized businesses identified a breach or attack in the previous 12 months in its 2025 edition.15

Dimension 4 · Lifecycle and supportability

Unsupported software is a baseline failure, not an advanced concern. CISA’s catalogue of Bad Practices lists the use of unsupported or end-of-life software as dangerous for all organizations.16 CIS safeguard 2.2, part of the essential-hygiene baseline, treats unsupported software as unauthorized unless there is a documented exception with mitigations.17 NIST CSF 2.0 frames the outcome as “Software is maintained, replaced, and removed commensurate with risk.”10

The open-source components inside applications age quietly. Black Duck’s 2025 analysis of 965 commercial codebases, audited mostly during acquisitions, found 90 percent contained components more than four years out of date.18

Item L4 brings in the TIME model, attributed to Gartner and widely used in application portfolio management: each application is classified as Tolerate, Invest, Migrate, or Eliminate, based on business value and technical fit.19

ItemScore
L1 ★Every critical application's operating system, runtime, and framework is vendor-supported, and end-of-support dates are recorded.012
L2Any unsupported software has a documented exception, mitigations, and an accepted owner of the risk.012
L3Third-party components are updated on a schedule, and none is more than four years out of date.012
L4Each application has a TIME classification, reviewed at least annually.012
L5Cloud and SaaS dependencies have a documented exit path.012

Dimension 5 · Cost

The cost of software arrives mostly after launch. Robert Glass’s widely cited synthesis puts maintenance at 40 to 80 percent of total software cost, averaging about 60 percent, and most of that is enhancement, the system keeping pace with the business.20 McKinsey’s 2020 survey of 50 CIOs found technical debt estimated at 20 to 40 percent of the value of the entire technology estate.21

Licence waste is the most visible symptom. Zylo, a vendor of SaaS management software, reported in its 2025 index that average licence utilization was about 47 percent, meaning roughly half of paid licences went unused.22

C3Technical debt is estimated for each critical application, with a budget to reduce it.012
C4A budget for planned change exists, separate from break-fix.012

Dimension 6 · AI components

This is where most estate assessments are silent and where risk is growing fastest. In Microsoft and LinkedIn’s 2024 Work Trend Index, 78 percent of people who use AI at work bring their own tools.23 IBM’s 2025 Cost of a Data Breach study found that 63 percent of breached organizations had no AI governance policy or were still developing one.24

AI components also have a lifecycle that is set by someone else. Anthropic commits to at least 60 days’ notice before retiring a model; OpenAI commits to at least six months for generally available models and as little as two weeks for previews.25 An AI feature without an owner stops working on a known date.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework makes each item below concrete. GOVERN 1.6 calls for mechanisms “to inventory AI systems.” GOVERN 1.7 calls for safe decommissioning. MANAGE 3.2 requires that pre-trained models “are monitored as part of AI system regular monitoring and maintenance.”26 ISO/IEC 42001 builds a certifiable management system around the same expectations.27

ItemScore
A1An inventory of AI components exists, including AI features in applications and tools staff have built.012
A2Each AI component has a named owner and documented responsibilities.012
A3Model versions are pinned, vendor retirement notices are monitored, and a fallback exists.012
A4The outputs of AI components are monitored after deployment.012
A5AI-generated code is reviewed and security-tested before it reaches production.012
A6Each AI feature can be switched off or retired without breaking the process it supports.012
A7A written policy covers which AI tools staff may use and with what data.012

Critical items. Whatever your total, a score of 0 on any of the four items marked ★ (O1 named owners, O4 company custody of accounts, R3 tested restore, L1 supported software) deserves action this quarter. Each one is a single point of failure that a good total can hide.

Reading your score

A scorecard is useful only if it changes what happens next. Three patterns are common.

Low Ownership, decent everything else. The practices exist because of one or two capable people. That is a strength today and a concentration risk tomorrow. Start with O1, O3, and O4: name the owners, give each critical application a second person, and move every account into company custody.

Good Ownership, low Recoverability or Lifecycle. The applications have owners who are fully occupied keeping them running. The gap is capacity, not accountability. Prioritize a tested restore (R3) and a supported-software plan (L1 and L2), then build the runbooks that let other people respond.

Low AI. This is now the most common pattern, including in companies that score well elsewhere. Begin with the inventory (A1) and owners (A2); the rest follows from knowing what exists.

In enterprise architecture terms, the scorecard is a lightweight baseline. TOGAF builds every change on a baseline description of the current state, and keeps delivered systems aligned with the business through architecture change management (Phase H). Re-scoring every six to twelve months, and recording each application’s TIME classification as you go, gives a mid-sized company the same discipline without the bureaucracy.

What to do in the next 90 days

  1. Score your three most critical applications, not just the estate as a whole. The differences between them will tell you where ownership is thin.
  2. Close the critical items. Name owners, move accounts into company custody, run and time a test restore, and record end-of-support dates.
  3. Classify each application with TIME. Decide what to invest in, what to tolerate on purpose, what to migrate, and what to retire.
  4. Assign the work. Each gap needs an owner and a date. Some will sit with your team; some are better carried by a partner.
  5. Re-score in six months and compare against your own baseline.
